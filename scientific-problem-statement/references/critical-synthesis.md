# Critical Synthesis and Scientific Argumentation

## Purpose
Transform primary findings into an explanation of what the collective evidence permits—and does not permit—researchers to conclude.

## Compare along meaningful axes
Contrast scientific question, endpoint/construct, design, assumptions, competing explanations, sample/data domain, controls, uncertainty, validation and intended application. Explicitly distinguish apparent disagreement due to incomparable contexts from substantive contradiction.

## Paragraph construction
A useful synthesis paragraph normally makes one claim, presents two or more meaningfully comparable lines of evidence where available, qualifies limitations, and states the justified inference. Avoid writing "A did X; B did Y; C did Z; therefore more research is needed."

Use **concession–limitation reasoning** when appropriate: fairly describe demonstrated achievements before pinpointing a consequential limitation. Do not force the rhetorical formula if another structure is clearer.

## Stress tests
- Is the alleged gap already answered in a competing paper?
- Does omitted variable/algorithm/experiment X plausibly alter a specified inference?
- Are the outcome and construct the same across studies?
- Is a difference in reported performance caused by different validation designs?
- Does the stated limitation actually prevent a conclusion, or only reflect the author's preference?
- What source would weaken the proposed argument?

## Output
A defensible analytical conclusion with calibrated language, not a high count of references. Each important comparison must lead to a scientific inference or reveal why such inference is not yet possible.
