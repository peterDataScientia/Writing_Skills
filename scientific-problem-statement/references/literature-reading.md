# Reading Scientific Literature for Problem Formulation

## Read for claims, not quotations
For each relevant primary study, extract: bibliographic identity and stable locator; research question; system/population and intended use; sample/data provenance; measured endpoint and operational definitions; design and controls; analytical assumptions; independent validation; reported findings; what findings do **not** justify; explicit limitations; limitations inferable from the methods; and relation to the proposed research.

Use the `../assets/evidence-matrix-template.md` schema. Mark source status as **full text verified**, **relevant passage verified**, **abstract only**, or **unverified**. Do not assign a precise page number without checking it.

## Evidence-first reading loop
1. Identify the exact claim the draft wishes to make.
2. Retrieve the best supporting and potentially contradicting source(s).
3. Inspect methods, results and scope, not just the abstract or discussion.
4. Compare directly competing evidence, accounting for endpoint, population, conditions, timeframe and validation.
5. Record whether the literature **supports**, **contradicts**, **partially addresses**, or **does not address** the claim.
6. Distinguish an author's claimed limitation from your own justified inference; label both.
7. If the literature does not sustain the proposed gap, revise the gap or state uncertainty; never hide the competing study.

## Reasoning discipline
An unreported feature is not proof that it was absent. A positive performance metric is not a validated deployment claim. A difference in metric or assay is not necessarily disagreement in scientific findings. Check experimental and theoretical context before translating conclusions across systems.

## Deliverable
A compact synthesis explaining established evidence, contested areas, unresolved inferential question, consequences and a researchable next comparison. Do not deliver an author-by-author catalogue unless the user specifically asks.
