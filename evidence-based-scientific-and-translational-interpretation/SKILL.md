# Evidence-Based Scientific and Translational Interpretation

## Purpose and scope

Interpret research **in any discipline**, without inventing significance. Explain (1) what was found, (2) what it means within the studied context, (3) which real problem or knowledge gap it addresses, (4) what it can reasonably inform, and (5) what remains unproven.

**General-purpose rule:** Never assume that the study concerns drugs, medicine, QSAR, networks, machine learning, or any particular field. Determine the discipline, study design, intended audience, research question, and type of evidence from the user's materials. Adapt domain terminology, standards and examples only after establishing that context.

"Translational" here means connecting knowledge to a justified use, practice, policy, technology, research decision, or societal application **when such a connection exists**. In some fields, the principal contribution is fundamental understanding, theory, measurement, interpretation or a better method. Do **not** manufacture practical or commercial applications to make research sound valuable.

## Activation

Use this skill when asked to:
- Explain what a result means, why it matters, or the "so what?" question.
- Draft or revise a scientific interpretation, Discussion, Significance, Implications, Impact or Conclusion.
- Explain research findings to specialists, practitioners, policymakers, students or the public.
- Assess whether a proposed application follows from the study's evidence.
- Critique exaggerated scientific claims, unsupported causal language or speculative benefits.
- Translate a set of results into research, design, policy or implementation decisions.

## Inputs and verification

Establish, where available:
1. Research problem, question, aims, theoretical or practical context.
2. Actual observations, estimates, figures, qualitative themes or textual evidence.
3. Population, sample, setting, period, measurement method and comparison group.
4. Study design (experiment, observation, simulation, modeling, qualitative study, review, theory, historical analysis, etc.).
5. Uncertainty, error, variation, sensitivity, limitations and alternative explanations.
6. Comparison with an appropriate baseline, prior work or status quo.
7. Intended audience and any decision or application the user wants to assess.

**Evidence first:** Read the provided data, tables, figures, quotations and source documents before making quantitative claims. If a critical item is missing, mark it as unavailable; never substitute a plausible value, fabricated result, imaginary mechanism or unverified citation. In source-based writing, distinguish direct quotations from your paraphrases.

## Mandatory interpretation workflow

### Step 1 — State the actual finding
Describe the result plainly and precisely: what changed, differed, co-occurred, was predicted, was observed or was argued? Include relevant direction, magnitude, units, timeframe, denominator, confidence or uncertainty when available. Do not confuse a method's output with independent confirmation.

### Step 2 — Identify the evidence level
Select the appropriate category, rather than assuming an experimental hierarchy is universal:
- **Descriptive:** distribution, pattern, trend, classification or network structure.
- **Associational:** relationship or covariation, without established causation.
- **Predictive:** estimated outcomes on appropriately evaluated unseen cases.
- **Causal/interventional:** effect supported by an adequate design and assumptions.
- **Mechanistic:** how a process operates, supported by direct or convergent evidence.
- **Qualitative/interpretive:** participants' accounts, themes, contextual meanings or textual interpretations with a transparent analytic basis.
- **Theoretical/mathematical:** deductions under stated axioms or assumptions.
- **Review/synthesis:** conclusions warranted by source quality, scope and synthesis method.

Evidence levels are **not** a single ranking across all disciplines. A rigorous qualitative finding can answer questions that a numerical model cannot.

### Step 3 — Explain the scientific or scholarly meaning
Explain what the finding teaches us about the *specific question*: a mechanism, conceptual distinction, pattern, constraint, measurement, model behavior, historical interpretation or theory. Note unexpected results, boundary conditions and competing explanations. Avoid restating the same result in grander language.

### Step 4 — Identify the real problem or gap
Describe the problem using documented context, not a made-up scenario. Ask:
- What was difficult to know, explain, predict, decide, design or implement before this work?
- Whose work or understanding could change?
- What does the result resolve, narrow, challenge or leave open?

If the result is basic research without an established application, say so and explain its knowledge contribution.

### Step 5 — Describe a warranted implication
Differentiate:
- **Demonstrated outcome:** directly observed or validated within the study.
- **Supported implication:** reasonably follows, with explicit scope and assumptions.
- **Potential application:** plausible but not yet tested.
- **Speculation:** lacks necessary evidence; omit or label unmistakably if relevant.

Identify a *specific* research, operational, educational, engineering, medical, social, legal, cultural or policy decision only when the evidence supports it. A result need not support any immediate action.

### Step 6 — Compare with existing knowledge
Use accurate, traceable sources and fair comparators. Explain whether the work replicates, extends, challenges or qualifies prior findings. A claim of improvement needs an appropriate baseline and equivalent evaluation conditions. Do not mistake novelty of wording, visualization or software for scientific novelty.

### Step 7 — Test inference boundaries
Check for leaps such as:
- Correlation → causation.
- Statistical significance → practical importance.
- Prediction → observed outcome.
- Simulation → real-world performance.
- Laboratory result → population or clinical benefit.
- Small or unrepresentative sample → universal conclusion.
- One location or time → all settings.
- Qualitative theme → population prevalence.
- Benchmark advantage → deployment success.
- Historical evidence → certainty about intent.
- Model explanation → proven physical mechanism.

State the missing evidence *only where relevant*, without turning every Discussion paragraph into a generic limitations list.

### Step 8 — Produce the interpretation
Default to a coherent paragraph, in this logical order:

**Verified finding → Meaning in the study → Relevant problem/gap → Justified implication or decision → Important boundary, if needed.**

Use plain, field-appropriate language. Give the practical meaning directly before specialist jargon if the audience is nontechnical. Preserve uncertainties without repeatedly using vague hedges.

## Claim–evidence ledger (internal quality control)

For each important conclusion, check:

| Question | Required answer |
| --- | --- |
| What is claimed? | Precisely worded proposition |
| Where is the evidence? | Named dataset, figure, table, interview, primary text, experiment or reliable source |
| What type of evidence? | Design and inference class |
| What comparator? | Relevant baseline or alternative, if applicable |
| What is justified? | Narrow interpretation and scope |
| What is not established? | Specific missing causal, predictive, contextual or implementation link |
| Why does it matter? | Knowledge contribution or validated decision relevance |

Do not output this table by default; use it to audit the final prose.

## Rules for quantitative work

- Preserve units, sample sizes, effect directions and denominators.
- Distinguish absolute and relative changes, correlation and agreement, accuracy and calibration, statistical and practical significance.
- Report variability or uncertainty where available.
- Check target leakage, dependence, sampling, confounding and external validity when relevant.
- Never turn a single metric into a broad claim of usefulness.
- When discussing predictive models, specify the validation conditions and intended use.

## Rules for qualitative, interpretive and theoretical work

- Ground themes and interpretations in actual excerpts, observations or analytic records; do not invent quotations.
- Preserve participants' meanings, social setting, competing readings and researcher positionality when relevant.
- Distinguish interpretive plausibility from quantified prevalence or experimentally established causation.
- For proofs and models, state premises, applicability and assumptions; do not equate formal validity with empirical confirmation.
- Recognize that an important theoretical or interpretive contribution may have no immediate implementation step.

## Translational claims: domain-neutral safeguards

Never claim improved health, safety, productivity, equity, learning outcomes, sustainability, economic return, policy effectiveness or social benefit without suitable evidence. Clearly distinguish:
1. Knowledge generated.
2. A possible way to use that knowledge.
3. Whether the application was actually tested.
4. Whether benefits were measured in the intended real setting.

For discipline-specific work, consult appropriate standards (e.g., reporting guidelines, engineering tolerances, education evaluation methods, causal inference assumptions, or clinical regulatory requirements). Do not impose one field's standards on another.

## Writing rules

- Be specific: replace “has huge potential” with the precise demonstrated implication.
- No invented case studies presented as real.
- No inflated novelty, certainty, impact, efficiency or cost-saving claims.
- Never force every finding into a commercial or clinical narrative.
- Explain negative, null and mixed findings as seriously as positive findings.
- Avoid unsupported recommendations and causal verbs.
- Separate Results (what was found) from Discussion (what it means), unless user requests integration.
- Use concise, natural paragraphs, smooth transitions and accurate citations.
- Keep the output proportionate to evidence strength and audience.
- If the user asks “what does this mean in reality?”, answer with a direct, concrete statement, not metaphors or motivational language.

## Examples across fields (illustrations only, not default assumptions)

**Chemistry/QSAR:** A molecular similarity network contains structurally close compounds with different measured potency. This identifies compound pairs for closer structural and assay review; it does not prove that the network improved a predictive model or produced an effective drug.

**Education:** A teaching intervention produced higher assessment scores in one study. This supports a benefit in the studied setting if the design rules out major confounding; it does not by itself establish nationwide effectiveness.

**Agriculture:** A fertilizer treatment increased average yield under specified conditions. Practical advice depends on soil type, weather, cost, environmental effects and replication across farms.

**Social science:** Institutional trust and civic participation were correlated. The finding describes an association in the studied population, not proof that raising trust causes participation to increase.

**Physics/engineering:** A device was more efficient in controlled tests. The result supports performance under those conditions; field reliability, maintenance and economics require separate evaluation.

**Humanities/history:** Multiple independent primary sources support a particular interpretation of an event. This advances historical understanding, not an experimentally testable claim about universal human behavior.

## Output modes

Choose the format requested by the user:
- **Plain-language answer:** 1–3 paragraphs answering what was learned and why it matters.
- **Manuscript-ready paragraph:** evidence-linked academic prose, no headings unless required.
- **Critical audit:** claim → evidence → overreach → corrected wording.
- **Concept/proposal significance:** documented problem, knowledge gap, proposed contribution, and what future study must demonstrate.
- **Policy/practice brief:** findings, affected setting, possible decision, implementation dependencies and uncertainties.

When the user supplies no exact results, provide an interpretation **framework**, not fabricated findings.

## Final quality gate

Before finalizing, verify all of the following:
1. The correct discipline and research question are identified.
2. Results are genuine, precisely represented and traceable.
3. No unstated mechanism, causal relationship or benefit is asserted.
4. Comparators and uncertainty are respected.
5. The practical problem is real and supported.
6. Potential applications are distinguished from demonstrated outcomes.
7. Negative and mixed evidence is not concealed.
8. No forced domain-specific jargon or clinical translation.
9. Language is clear enough for the intended audience.
10. The answer genuinely explains **“what did we learn, and why does that matter?”**

## Reference principles

The skill is informed by common research-writing and inference principles rather than restricted to a fixed set of papers. When a project requires a literature-supported interpretation, retrieve and verify the **relevant domain literature** and cite the primary sources used. Do not treat examples from chemistry or QSAR as universal methodological authority.
