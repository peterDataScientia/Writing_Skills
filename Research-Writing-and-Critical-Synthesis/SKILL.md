# Research Writing and Critical Synthesis

## Purpose

Write research arguments rather than compilations of studies. Help the reader understand **what matters, what is known, how we know it, what is uncertain, why the proposed question matters, and what the study can reasonably contribute**. Apply across scientific, engineering, educational, social-science, health, computational, and interdisciplinary research.

The final manuscript should sound like a researcher interpreting evidence—not an assistant reciting citations, apologizing for limitations, or selling methods.

## When to use

- Draft or critique **Background of the Study**, **Statement of the Problem**, **Significance**, **Literature Review**, **Research Gap**, or transitions between them.
- Audit whether a research proposal's claims and methods actually respond to its **research questions (RQs)**.
- Edit a thesis or manuscript to improve scientific progression, analytical interpretation, stakeholders' practical value, and concision.
- Compare exemplary dissertations or primary papers without copying their wording or treating an individual author's conventions as universal.

## Non-negotiable operating rules

1. **Read the source.** When a manuscript, thesis, or article is supplied, inspect the relevant full section. Do not substitute a past summary for unavailable text. Explicitly distinguish source findings, the author's interpretation, and the present writer's inference.
2. **Evidence before rhetoric.** Verify consequential experimental values, units, endpoints, disease models, and study designs before repeating them. Mark unverified facts as requiring confirmation; never invent citations, numerical results, assay details, or novelty.
3. **Novelty is comparative and bounded.** Never assert "no studies have..." without an appropriately comprehensive and documented search. Distinguish established methods, distinct populations/endpoints, and unanswered applications.
4. **The question controls the argument.** Every section serves the research aim and RQs; do not retrofit RQs to justify a fashionable technique.
5. **Interpret, don't merely report.** Authors' voice emerges from reasoned comparisons, meaningful distinctions, implications, and limits grounded in the cited evidence.
6. **Don't overqualify every sentence.** Introduce a genuinely material qualification where it changes interpretation, then move forward. Avoid repeated "however", "nevertheless", and defensive caveats.
7. **Respect study stage.** In a proposal, say what *will be assessed* or *may provide*; do not write as though planned experiments have already yielded results.
8. **Respect causal boundaries.** Association, model prediction, binding, enzymatic inhibition, cellular phenotypes, animal outcomes, and clinical benefit are different evidentiary levels.
9. **One paragraph, one advancing job.** Remove repeated claims and empty summary sentences. Maintain natural transitions; do not add a paragraph merely because a citation exists.
10. **Preserve document integrity.** If editing a file, maintain unaffected wording, citations, figure numbering, equations, references, and formatting. For tracked color revisions, color only the actually changed phrases, not whole unchanged paragraphs.
11. **General purpose.** Examples below illustrate reasoning patterns, not wording to paste or a preferred topic.

## Step 1 — Establish the research argument before writing

Extract from the available proposal/brief:

| Anchor | Question to answer |
|---|---|
| Real-world or disciplinary problem | What is at stake, for whom, and on what evidence? |
| Study object/system | What entity, process, population, or outcome is under study? |
| What is established | Which primary studies directly support the present rationale? |
| What remains uncertain | Exactly which context, mechanism, endpoint, relationship, or decision is unresolved? |
| RQs/objectives | What observable answer would resolve each question? |
| Method–question fit | Why can the proposed data, experiments, or analyses answer those RQs? |
| Stakeholders | Who might use which specific output to make which decision? |
| Claim ceiling | What cannot be inferred from the planned evidence? |

Create an internal **claim–evidence–implication map**:

`Claim → best evidence + endpoint/context → comparison/qualification → implication for this study`.

If there is no evidence for a strong claim, weaken or remove it; do not invent a paper.

## Step 2 — Write the Background: broad to specific, without becoming generic

Use a *logical funnel*; the number of paragraphs varies by discipline.

1. **Problem and stakes:** Establish the consequential problem with pertinent, recent, accurate evidence.
2. **Relevant process/system:** Explain the disciplinary mechanism, context, or theoretical basis that connects the problem to the study object.
3. **Current approaches and achievements:** Show what researchers or practitioners already do; acknowledge successful work accurately.
4. **Specific unresolved issue:** Explain the missing knowledge, uncertain relationship, transferability problem, or implementation barrier.
5. **Proposed direction:** Indicate why the planned investigation is an appropriate next scientific step, without turning the Background into a methods inventory.

**Paragraph discipline**
- Begin with a claim/topic, not a citation parade.
- Include selected evidence and its relevant endpoint or context.
- Interpret why it matters *here*.
- Finish by leading logically to the next question.
- Compress when successive paragraphs repeat the same rationale.

**Avoid**
- Starting with "Machine learning is revolutionary..." when the real problem is clinical, chemical, educational, or environmental.
- Presenting the proposed method as the research problem.
- Overloading the Background with benchmark metrics, algorithm menus, or full workflow steps better addressed in literature or methods.
- Claiming an early experimental finding has proven clinical effectiveness.

**General illustration**: If an existing prediction method works in some conditions, a background can motivate studying reliability under changed sample conditions, rather than pretending that prediction itself was never attempted. This is a structural lesson illustrated by Mgaya's 2022 MSc dissertation, not a reusable claim about every research field.

## Step 3 — Write the Statement of the Problem: a precise unresolved question

The Statement should be narrower than the Background, and not repeat it.

**Reasoning sequence:**
`Consequential problem → existing knowledge/capability → specific unresolved limitation or uncertainty → its consequence for knowledge or decisions → what this study needs to establish.`

Check:
- Could a reader identify the problem *without seeing the title*?
- Is the gap a **researchable uncertainty**, not merely the absence of a favorite tool?
- Does the gap survive acknowledgement of closely related published work?
- Is there a measurable outcome or comparison?
- Does it align with at least one RQ, and jointly with the study aims?

**Weak:** "Previous studies used docking but did not use our chosen algorithm."

**Stronger reasoning:** "The key unresolved issue is whether estimates generalize to the intended chemical population and provide useful guidance for an experimentally testable selection decision."

This is a generic **reasoning example**, not an established literature claim for a particular field.

Do not manufacture a "gap" by belittling earlier studies for not pursuing objectives they never claimed.

## Step 4 — Write the Significance: value to identifiable users, within evidence limits

Distinguish three complementary levels:

- **Scientific significance:** Which knowledge, relationship, dataset, mechanism, or evidence will become clearer?
- **Methodological significance:** What valid comparison, model, protocol, benchmark, or reliability assessment will be provided?
- **Practical/stakeholder significance:** **Who can use what output for which real decision or subsequent work?**

Prefer this compact progression:

`Research contribution → usable outputs → named stakeholder(s) and practical use → realistic downstream opportunity.`

The key stakeholder test:

> "For [specific stakeholder], [particular study output] can support [specific research/practice decision], subject to [material scope limit, if needed]."

**Do not** merely list "scientists, policymakers, industry, and society" without explaining their different uses.

**Do not** confuse "we will compare methods" with why the study matters. Name the decision enabled by the comparison.

**Avoid speculative promises:** model evaluation does not itself create a new medicine, eliminate disease, generate employment, improve national policy, or prove clinical efficacy. The contribution must be proportionate to actual methods.

**Lesson from contrasting sources:** Lucas Paul's 2022 PhD thesis foregrounds practical beneficiaries and applications; its breadth is a useful rhetorical lesson, but some far-reaching outcomes require stronger direct evidence. Mgaya's 2022 MSc dissertation keeps significance close to a specific methodological problem but is relatively brief on downstream stakeholders. Combine clarity of use with defensible scope.

## Step 5 — Write Literature Review as critical synthesis, not annotated bibliography

### Reading protocol for each key primary study

Extract:
- Author/year and the actual **research question**
- Population, material, compounds, model, or setting
- Dataset size and provenance where consequential
- Method and comparison conditions
- Measured endpoint(s), units, and whether outcomes are predicted or observed
- Principal results *as published*, including inconsistencies across abstract/results/tables
- Conclusions explicitly drawn by the authors
- Your bounded interpretation and relevance to the present RQ

**Never equate** docking score with IC50, regression with classification, in vitro activity with clinical benefit, a lysate assay with intact-cell engagement, or predicted candidates with experimentally confirmed hits.

### Synthesis unit

Organize by **scientific question, mechanism, approach, or evidence level** rather than "Author A did X. Author B did Y. Author C did Z."

Within a paragraph:
1. State the **comparative theme**.
2. Supply 1–3 strategically selected studies with enough methodological/quantitative detail to compare properly.
3. Explain **what the comparison establishes** and what it does not.
4. Bridge to the next *distinct* question.

**Author's analytical voice is heard in sentences such as:**
- "The difference in measured potency indicates that dual activity need not imply comparable inhibitory strength."
- "These approaches answer different questions: one prioritizes possible binders, while another estimates the magnitude of an assay-defined response."
- "The performance claim is conditional on the population and validation design used."
- "The experimental results support a biochemical effect; the reported cellular phenotype is a distinct endpoint."

These are *illustrative reasoning patterns*: verify source support before applying them.

### Avoid robotic critique

Bad: "However, the study had limitations... Furthermore, another study... However..."

Better: "The first study established X under condition A, whereas the second extended Y to condition B. Their endpoints are not directly comparable; together they motivate testing Z."

Critique only when it changes how evidence transfers to the present study. Balance strengths and boundaries. A literature review should display intellectual ownership, not repeated disclaimers.

### Quantitative modelling: argue its actual value, not false superiority

Where relevant, distinguish:

- **Classification:** predicted membership in an activity category.
- **Regression/potency modelling:** continuous estimates of an assay-defined response (e.g., IC50/pIC50).
- **Docking:** structural or scoring-based prioritization under an approximate model.
- **Pharmacophore methods:** matching spatial/interaction features of known active ligands.

Quantitative prediction may:
1. Characterize a **structure–response relationship**;
2. Estimate **relative and absolute response magnitude**, subject to calibration and error;
3. Inform **compound/sample prioritization or optimization**;
4. Be evaluated through **prospective comparison** with measured outcomes.

Do **not** claim all regression models are mechanistically interpretable: e.g., nonlinear fingerprint models and 3D field contour models support different degrees of explanation.

Use a comparison of actual publications to establish this value; do not suggest other approaches failed merely because their aims were different.

## Step 6 — Write Research Gap as the result of synthesis

Choose a heading that suits the institution and section contents:
- `Research Gap`
- `Synthesis of Literature and Research Gap`
- `Critical Synthesis and Research Gap` only if a combined analytical discussion is genuinely necessary.

No heading is inherently superior. The paragraphs, not the heading, demonstrate analytical voice.

A strong final synthesis:
1. Acknowledges what the literature **already establishes**.
2. Defines the remaining uncertainty in terms of **population, context, endpoint, transferability, explanatory mechanism, or intended decision**.
3. Explains why resolving it **matters**.
4. Connects to specific RQs and an appropriate evaluation strategy.

Avoid repeating every algorithm, assay, limitation, and contribution mentioned earlier. Often one or two dense paragraphs are better than three repetitive ones.

## Step 7 — Align sections without duplicating their language

| Section | Single dominant job |
|---|---|
| Background | **Why this problem and direction matter** |
| Problem Statement | **What precise uncertainty remains** |
| Objectives / RQs | **What the study will determine** |
| Significance | **Who benefits from what credible output and how** |
| Literature Review | **What earlier evidence establishes and how it compares** |
| Research Gap | **What remains unanswered after synthesis** |
| Methods | **How the questions will be answered and evaluated** |

Use the same conceptual spine, **not the same sentences**, across sections.

### RQ-to-value audit

For each RQ, fill:
`RQ → expected evidence/output → scientific meaning → practical decision or subsequent study enabled → limits`.

If a significance claim does not map to an actual output, revise it.
If the methods cannot yield the claimed evidence, reduce the claim or flag a design gap.

## Step 8 — Edit for human, concise, authoritative scholarly prose

### Remove
- Generic openings: "In today's rapidly evolving world..."
- Signposting that announces analysis instead of doing it: "This clearly highlights the importance of..."
- Repeated "These findings demonstrate...", especially in every paragraph.
- Empty novelty slogans: "For the first time..." without a search.
- Self-critical boilerplate: "This study has several limitations" when discussing somebody else's paper without a specific reason.
- Method inventories in Significance.
- Repeated safety qualifications already established once.
- Citation stacks that lack accompanying interpretation.

### Prefer
- Concrete nouns, named endpoints, named populations, measured values.
- Verbs that distinguish evidentiary levels: *reported, observed, estimated, predicted, associated with, supported, suggested*.
- Parallel, precise comparisons.
- Causal language only when warranted by intervention design.
- One justified interpretation per meaningful group of findings.
- Realistic downstream applications, never premature clinical or societal promises.

### Preserve technical precision
- Exact experimental vs predicted distinctions.
- Concentration units, transformed endpoint definitions, domain specificity, confidence intervals, and experimental conditions when relevant.
- Correct study names, compound identifiers, authorship, publication years.
- Contradictions inside a source should be identified and checked; do not silently reconcile incompatible numbers.

## Revision protocol for an existing Word manuscript

1. Inspect the **latest explicitly approved document**, not an earlier artifact.
2. Identify exact paragraphs/sentences to change; record section, original, replacement, and rationale.
3. Preserve unaffected text, headings, references, citation hyperlinks, equations, captions, and table contents.
4. For color-coded revisions: **only inserted/replaced spans red**; unchanged spans stay in their original color. Do not mark the whole paragraph red when only a sentence changed.
5. Do not insert new sources or replace published values without verification.
6. Verify that modifications were applied, reference formatting remained consistent, and the document renders without broken layout.
7. Report precisely what changed; distinguish a written proposal from edits actually saved.

## Quality-control checklist

Use this as an internal pass/fail rubric, not decorative checklist text in a manuscript.

- [ ] The first substantive Background paragraph makes the actual problem clear.
- [ ] Literature is used to support **arguments**, not as an author-by-author list.
- [ ] Every consequential comparison respects methods, context, and endpoints.
- [ ] The research gap is narrow, testable, and not based on an invented absence.
- [ ] The Statement of the Problem does not re-tell the whole Background.
- [ ] The author's voice is evident in grounded interpretation, without repeated caveats.
- [ ] Each RQ maps to available/planned evidence.
- [ ] Significance identifies **stakeholders, outputs, and concrete decisions**.
- [ ] Practical value does not overclaim downstream effects.
- [ ] Methods and claims are consistent with proposal vs completed-study tense.
- [ ] Each section adds something distinct; duplicate potency/utility/validation arguments are cut.
- [ ] Citations and numerical facts are checked against original sources when available.
- [ ] Edits preserve source integrity; only genuine changes are highlighted when requested.
- [ ] The final prose reads naturally when citations are temporarily hidden.

## Optional output formats

When asked to **review**, provide:
`Section | Evidence-based strength | Precise weakness | Why it matters | Proposed revision`, then a concise prioritized verdict.

When asked to **rewrite**, provide the complete section with original heading and proper citations; do not mix commentary into the manuscript.

When asked to **compare exemplars**, distinguish:
`What author wrote → how the argument works → transferable writing principle → what not to copy`.

When asked to **mark changes**, provide an edited DOCX and a brief change log; changed phrases only should be visually marked.
