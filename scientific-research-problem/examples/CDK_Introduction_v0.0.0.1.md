# 1. Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major global health challenge, accounting for approximately 2.4 million new diagnoses and 694,000 deaths worldwide in 2024 (WHO, 2026). The disease comprises a heterogeneous group of malignancies that predominantly originate in the epithelial cells of the breast ducts or lobules. Depending on whether malignant cells have penetrated the surrounding breast tissue, breast cancer may be classified as non-invasive or invasive. Invasive breast cancer can spread to regional lymph nodes and distant organs, with the extent of disease commonly described using a staging system ranging from stage I to stage IV, while stage 0 generally denotes in situ disease. As the disease progresses from localised to locally advanced or metastatic stages, therapeutic management becomes increasingly complex. This complexity is further influenced by the biological characteristics of the tumour, particularly its hormone receptor and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 expression status, which guide the selection of systemic treatment.

Based on hormone receptor (HR) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) status, breast cancer is clinically classified into four major subtypes: HR+/HER2−, HR+/HER2+, HR−/HER2+ and HR−/HER2−. These subtypes differ in the molecular pathways that regulate tumour growth and, consequently, in their responsiveness to systemic therapies. In HR+/HER2− breast cancer, which represents the most commonly diagnosed subtype, tumour cell proliferation is frequently dependent on oestrogen receptor signalling. Activation of this signalling pathway promotes the expression of genes involved in cell growth and division, providing the biological basis for the use of endocrine therapy. By suppressing oestrogen production or interfering with oestrogen receptor activity, endocrine therapy seeks to restrict the proliferative signals that sustain tumour growth. However, the effectiveness of this therapeutic approach also depends on the ability of cancer cells to regulate the molecular machinery responsible for cell-cycle progression.

Cell-cycle progression is regulated by a family of enzymes known as cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), whose activities are controlled through interactions with regulatory proteins called cyclins. In humans, this family comprises 20 members, designated CDK1 to CDK20, which participate in diverse cellular processes, including cell-cycle regulation and gene transcription. Although these enzymes share structural similarities, they perform distinct biological functions, and their numerical designations do not necessarily reflect the sequence of their activities during the cell cycle. Among the cell-cycle-regulating members, CDK4 and CDK6 primarily regulate progression through the first gap phase (G1), CDK2 facilitates the transition from G1 to the DNA synthesis phase (S), and CDK1 is essential for entry into mitosis. The coordinated activities of these kinases ensure that cells progress through successive stages of division under appropriate physiological conditions. In hormone-responsive breast cancer cells, oestrogen receptor signalling can promote the expression of cyclin D, thereby stimulating CDK4 and CDK6 activity and facilitating subsequent CDK2-mediated cell-cycle progression. This connection between hormonal signalling and cell-cycle regulation provides the biological rationale for combining endocrine therapy with CDK4/6 inhibition.

The role of CDK4 and CDK6 in regulating cell division provides the basis for combining their inhibition with endocrine therapy in HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer. While endocrine therapy suppresses oestrogen-dependent growth signals, CDK4/6 inhibitors restrict tumour cells from progressing towards DNA replication. Three widely used inhibitors, palbociclib, ribociclib and abemaciclib, have improved clinical outcomes in this patient population. For example, the addition of ribociclib to letrozole increased median overall survival from 51.4 to 63.9 months in the phase III MONALEESA-2 trial (Hortobagyi et al., 2022). Despite these improvements, some patients do not respond to treatment, whereas others initially respond but subsequently experience disease progression. These clinical outcomes are consistent with primary and acquired treatment resistance, respectively. One mechanism through which tumour cells can escape CDK4/6 inhibition involves increased cyclin E–CDK2 activity, which allows cells to continue progressing through the cell cycle.

Although resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors can arise through several mechanisms, increased cyclin E–CDK2 activity is particularly relevant to the development of alternative kinase inhibitors. Cyclin E activates CDK2, which can promote retinoblastoma protein phosphorylation and sustain cell-cycle progression even when CDK4 and CDK6 are inhibited. The clinical relevance of this mechanism is supported by findings from the PALOMA-3 trial, in which patients receiving palbociclib plus fulvestrant had a median progression-free survival of 7.6 months in tumours with high cyclin E1 expression, compared with 14.1 months in those with low expression (Turner et al., 2019). This association suggests that increased cyclin E1 expression may limit the therapeutic benefit of CDK4/6 inhibition. Further evidence was provided by Dietrich et al. (2024), who demonstrated that selective CDK2 inhibition restored cell-cycle control in preclinical models of CDK4/6 inhibitor-resistant breast cancer. Together, these findings provide a rationale for combining CDK2 inhibition with CDK4/6 inhibition to address resistance associated with increased cyclin E–CDK2 activity.

The recognition of CDK2 activity as a mechanism of resistance to CDK4/6 inhibitors has prompted the development of compounds capable of simultaneously inhibiting CDK2, CDK4 and CDK6. One such compound, PF-06873600, demonstrated antitumour activity in preclinical models of breast cancer resistant to CDK4/6 inhibition, supporting its subsequent clinical investigation in patients with HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer. Although preliminary clinical findings indicated antitumour activity, haematological adverse effects remained a concern, including neutropenia, a reduction in the number of circulating neutrophils that increases susceptibility to infections. The persistence of haematological toxicity despite the expansion of kinase inhibition to include CDK2 raises the question of whether all three kinases need to be inhibited simultaneously to achieve the desired therapeutic effect.

The question of whether CDK4 and CDK6 need to be inhibited simultaneously has led to investigations into their individual contributions to tumour proliferation and treatment-associated toxicity. Although both kinases participate in cell-cycle regulation, their relative importance differs across cell types. In certain HR+/HER2− breast cancer models, tumour proliferation is predominantly dependent on CDK4, whereas CDK6 plays an important role in the production of blood cells. This functional distinction provided the basis for the development of atirmociclib, a preferential CDK4 inhibitor designed to minimise CDK6 inhibition. Palmer et al. (2025) reported that preferential CDK4 inhibition reduced haematological toxicity in preclinical models, allowing greater drug exposure and improved antitumour responses. These findings, together with evidence supporting CDK2 inhibition in CDK4/6 inhibitor-resistant breast cancer, provide a rationale for investigating compounds that simultaneously inhibit CDK2 and CDK4 while retaining comparatively weak inhibitory activity against CDK6.

Concurrent inhibition of CDK2 and CDK4 with reduced inhibitory activity against CDK6 has been investigated through both drug combinations and the development of dual-target inhibitors. The combination approach is exemplified by the phase Ib/II clinical investigation of tagtociclib, a selective CDK2 inhibitor, and atirmociclib, a preferential CDK4 inhibitor, in patients with HR+/HER2− advanced breast cancer (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT05262400). In pursuing the single-molecule approach, Bai et al. (2025) reported that INV-6452 suppressed proliferation in palbociclib-resistant breast cancer models in which selective inhibition of either CDK2 or CDK4 alone showed limited activity. Further evidence of the feasibility of preferentially targeting CDK2 and CDK4 was provided by the development of Cpd-1308, which exhibited cellular target-engagement IC₅₀ values of 123, 24 and 521 nM against CDK2, CDK4 and CDK6, respectively (AACR, 2026). These values correspond to approximately 4.2-fold and 21.7-fold selectivity for CDK2 and CDK4 over CDK6, respectively, illustrating that the degree of CDK6 sparing may differ considerably between the two intended targets.

The unequal selectivity observed for Cpd-1308 illustrates the difficulty of achieving sufficient selectivity for both CDK2 and CDK4 over CDK6 within a single molecule. This difficulty arises partly from the structural conservation of the ATP-binding sites through which many CDK inhibitors exert their activity. In particular, CDK2, CDK4 and CDK6 share conserved structural elements, including the catalytic lysine, phenylalanine gatekeeper residue, glycine-rich loop and hinge region, which contribute to ATP binding and molecular recognition by inhibitors. The structural similarity is particularly pronounced between CDK4 and CDK6, whose ATP-binding sites exhibit approximately 92% sequence identity. Despite this conservation, differences in amino acid composition, binding-site geometry and conformational dynamics provide opportunities for selective inhibition, as illustrated by the development of the preferential CDK4 inhibitor atirmociclib. Exploiting these differences to develop CDK6-sparing CDK2/CDK4 dual inhibitors requires identifying chemical features that favour inhibitory activity against both CDK2 and CDK4 without conferring comparable activity against CDK6. Consequently, compound discovery for this pharmacological profile requires the simultaneous evaluation of inhibitory potency against CDK2 and CDK4 and their respective selectivity over CDK6.

Experimental approaches to identifying compounds with a desired kinase selectivity profile commonly involve compound screening, structural modification and subsequent evaluation of inhibitory activity against multiple kinases. Such evaluations are important because inhibitory activity against an intended kinase does not necessarily exclude activity against other structurally related kinases. For instance, Davis et al. (2011) profiled 72 kinase inhibitors against 442 kinases, revealing diverse patterns of selectivity across the human kinome. The experimental data generated through such investigations provide a basis for identifying relationships between chemical structure and kinase inhibitory activity. These relationships can be investigated using quantitative structure–activity relationship (QSAR) models, which relate molecular characteristics to experimentally measured biological activities. By learning from existing experimental data, these models can estimate the inhibitory activities of previously untested compounds, thereby supporting the prioritisation of candidates for subsequent experimental evaluation.

The ability of QSAR models to estimate inhibitory activity from molecular structures has encouraged their application in the discovery of selective CDK inhibitors. Kaveh et al. (2024) employed machine learning to investigate structure–activity and selectivity relationships among 8,592 compounds targeting CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK5 and CDK9, identifying molecular properties associated with inhibitory activity and selectivity across these kinases. Building on the application of machine learning to kinase selectivity, Akinnusi et al. (2026) developed a computational workflow that incorporated activity predictions for CDK1, CDK2, CDK4, CDK6, CDK7 and CDK9 to prioritise compounds exhibiting preferential CDK2 inhibition. Whereas their approach prioritised activity against CDK2 relative to other CDKs, Wang et al. (2026) investigated simultaneous inhibition of CDK4 and CDK6 using machine learning-guided virtual screening, identifying a compound with experimentally determined IC₅₀ values of 3.5 and 17.4 nM against CDK4 and CDK6, respectively. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the utility of machine learning in identifying compounds with different CDK inhibition profiles. However, the selection criteria employed in these investigations differ from those required to identify compounds that maintain sufficient inhibitory activity against both CDK2 and CDK4 while minimising activity against CDK6.

The distinction between these inhibition profiles is important because a compound with high predicted inhibitory activity against CDK2 may exhibit insufficient activity against CDK4, whereas a compound predicted to inhibit both kinases may also exhibit considerable activity against CDK6. Identifying CDK6-sparing dual inhibitors therefore requires selection criteria that account for the predicted potency of each intended kinase and its selectivity over CDK6. The importance of evaluating selectivity across kinase targets has been demonstrated in previous QSAR investigations (Martin & Zhu, 2011). The reliability of such evaluations depends on the accuracy of the individual activity predictions because errors in these predictions can affect the estimated differences in inhibitory potency. Moreover, the reliability of QSAR predictions depends partly on the chemical space represented by the training data, making applicability-domain assessment important when predicting the activities of previously untested compounds (Tropsha, 2010; OECD, 2023). Accordingly, this study integrates target-specific machine learning models, applicability-domain assessment and selectivity-based virtual screening to identify compounds with sufficient inhibitory activity against CDK2 and CDK4 and comparatively weaker activity against CDK6, with prioritised candidates intended for subsequent biochemical evaluation.
